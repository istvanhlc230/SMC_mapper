CHAPTER 1: FOUNDATION

Forex
Forex ?Foreign Exchange) is the global financial market where currencies are traded (bought and sold).

In simple terms, it is the market where one currency is exchanged for another. If you believe one currency will become stronger than another, you can trade based on that expectation.

If your analysis is correct, you make a profit. If you are wrong, you take a loss.

Real-Life Example

To understand Forex, think about traveling to another country.

When you travel, you usually exchange your local currency for the currency of the country you are visiting.

For example:

? You exchange your money at an airport or exchange booth
? You receive a different currency based on an exchange rate

That exchange rate represents the value of one currency compared to another. If you return later and the exchange rate has changed, you may notice that:
? You receive more money than before
? Or less money than before

This change in value is what creates opportunity in Forex trading.










6

Not all activity in the Forex market happens for the same reason.

In the real world, currencies are mainly exchanged for practical purposes such as international trade, tourism & travel, and business or cross-border payments.

These transactions are part of the "real economy,? where money is exchanged to support goods and services.

However, this is only a small part of what happens in the Forex market. The majority of trading activity is driven by speculation.
Speculation is when traders try to make money by buying a currency when they expect it to rise in value, or selling it when they expect it to fall in value, then closing the trade at a favorable price.

In other words, traders are not exchanging money because they need it for travel or business.

They are participating because they believe one currency will increase in value compared to another or vice versa

And they aim to profit from that movement.



The Forex market is the largest financial market in the world, with daily trading activity reaching trillions of dollars.

To put this into perspective, it is larger than all major stock markets combined in terms of daily trading volume. Currencies are being exchanged across the globe every second of the trading week.

This level of activity is possible because the market is global, decentralized, and made up of many different participants including banks, financial institutions, corporations, governments, and traders.
Aside from its size, the market also rarely closes. It stays active 24 hours a day, five days a week, pausing only when the weekend begins.




7

Unlike stock or bond markets that open and close at fixed times each day, Forex does not have a single central trading session. There is no official opening bell or closing bell.

Instead, trading activity shifts continuously from one financial center to another. When one major region becomes less active, another takes over, keeping the market active throughout the day. This rotation between different parts of the world is what allows trading to continue without interruption during the week.


Forex Trading
Forex Trading is the process of buying one currency while simultaneously selling another.

Currencies are always traded in pairs, meaning you are not trading a single currency on its own. Instead, you are trading the value of one currency against another. These pairs are quoted through a Forex broker, which gives you access to the global currency market.

For example, EUR/USD represents the euro against the US dollar, while GBP/USD represents the British pound against the US dollar.

Every currency pair represents a comparison between two economies. As one currency strengthens or weakens against the other, the price of the pair moves up or down.

In Forex, currency pairs are usually grouped into three categories:

Major pairs, minor pairs, and exotic pairs.

Major pairs always include the US dollar and are the most widely traded in the market. Because of this, they tend to have the highest level of activity and the most stable pricing behavior compared to other categories.

This is largely because the United States has the largest economy in the world, and the US dollar plays a central role in global trade and finance.

Examples of major pairs include EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/JPY, USD/CHF, USD/CAD, AUD/USD, and NZD/USD.

Minor pairs are currency pairs that do not include the US dollar but involve other major currencies such as the euro, British pound, or Japanese yen.


8

Exotic pairs consist of one major currency paired with a currency from a developing or smaller economy. These pairs are generally less actively traded compared to majors and minors.

Currency prices change based on how much demand there is for one currency compared to another.

When more traders or institutions want to buy a currency, its value tends to rise. When more people want to sell it, its value tends to fall.

This constant shift in buying and selling pressure is what creates movement in the Forex market.

Some currency pairs are traded more than others. When a pair is traded frequently by many participants, it is considered more active.

Major pairs like EUR/USD are more active compared to less commonly traded pairs like AUD/NZD. This difference affects how smoothly and frequently prices move.

Bid, Ask, and Spread

Bid

The bid is the price at which your broker is willing to buy the base currency in exchange for the quote currency

This means the bid is the best available price at which you (the trader) can sell to the market.

If you want to sell something, the broker will buy it from you at the bid price.

Ask

The ask is the price at which your broker will sell the base currency in exchange for the quote currency.

This means the ask price is the best available price at which you can buy from the market. If you want to buy something, the broker will sell (or offer) it to you at the ask price.
Spread

The difference between the bid and the ask price is known as the SPREAD. In general, the bid is lower than the ask price.
Considering the image above:

If you want to sell GBP/USD, your trade will be executed at the bid price of 1.34587. If you want to buy GBP/USD, your trade will be executed at the ask price of 1.34588.
Notice that the buy price is slightly higher than the sell price. This small difference is the spread, which represents the cost of entering a trade (transaction cost).

In this example:

Spread = 1.34588 - 1.34587 = 0.00001

This equals 0.1 pip (or 1 pipette).

Every trade begins with this small cost, meaning the market must move in your favor by at least the spread amount before the position becomes profitable.

For most currency pairs, a pip is the fourth decimal place.

If EUR/USD moves from 1.1650 ? 1.1660, that 0.0010 USD rise in value is 10 pips

What is Leverage?

One of the unique features of the Forex market is the ability to trade large positions with a relatively small amount of money. This is known as leverage.

Leverage is the ratio of the amount of capital used in a transaction to the required security deposit ( the "margin").

Leverage allows a trader to control a larger position than the amount of capital available in their account.
For example, if a broker offers leverage of 100?1, a trader can control a position worth
$100,000 using only $1,000 of their own funds.

Leverage varies dramatically with different brokers, ranging from 2?1 to Unlimited:1. Think of leverage as a tool that increases your buying power.
15

Without leverage, opening large positions would require significant amounts of capital. With leverage, traders can participate in larger trades while committing only a fraction of the total position value.

However, leverage is a double-edged sword.

While it can increase profits, it can also increase losses by the same proportion.

What is Margin?
Margin is the amount of money required to open and maintain a leveraged position. When a trade is opened, a portion of your account balance is set aside by the broker as a
security deposit. This amount is known as margin. For example:
If a broker requires 1% margin and you open a position worth $100,000, you would need
$1,000 in your account to open that trade.

Its purpose is simply to ensure that sufficient funds exist to support the position while it remains open.

Leverage and margin work together.

Higher leverage means less margin is required to control a position. For example:

Types of Orders

? Pending Orders

A market order is executed immediately at the best available price, while a pending order is executed only when price reaches a level specified by the trader.

Market Orders

A market order is an instruction to buy or sell immediately at the current market price.

For example, if EUR/USD is currently trading at 1.1050 and you decide to enter a trade right away, you would use a market order.

Market orders are the fastest way to enter the market because execution happens instantly.

Market Orders include:

? Buy Market Order
? Sell Market Order

A Buy Market Order is used when you believe price will rise. A Sell Market Order is used when you believe price will fall.



Pending Orders

A pending order allows you to enter a trade automatically when price reaches a specific level in the future.

How Forex Brokers Allow You to Trade Any Currency Pair

?USD?.

What is a Candlestick (in Forex)?

A candlestick is a visual representation of price movement within a specific timeframe. Each candlestick shows how price changed during that period.


Parts of a Candlestick

Every candlestick is made up of two main components: the body and the wicks (also called shadows).
? Body - the range between the opening and closing price
? Wicks (shadows) - the highest and lowest price reached during that period

Together, these elements give a complete picture of what happened within a single timeframe.

Every candlestick contains four key pieces of information:

? Open ?O? - the price at the start of the period
? Close ?C? - the price at the end of the period
? High ?H? - the highest price reached
? Low ?L? - the lowest price reached

Types of Candlesticks

Candlesticks are generally classified into two main types:

? Bullish candlestick - indicates buyers were stronger during that period
? Bearish candlestick - indicates sellers were stronger during that period

29



Candlesticks do not predict the market on their own. Instead, they show the interaction between buyers and sellers during a specific period of time.




A Candlestick-based uptrend exists when price breaks above the high of the previous bullish candle while the low is protected. The uptrend continues until a low of a bullish candle is taken out.


A Candlestick-based downtrend exists when price breaks below the low of the previous bearish candle while the high is protected. The downtrend continues until a high of a bearish candle is taken out.

A bullish valid pullback occurs when, within a Candlestick-based uptrend, price first takes out the low of the previous bullish candle by either a wick or a body, whether that happens in a single candle or across multiple candles, and regardless of candle color, after which price reverses and breaks above the high of that same previous bullish candle to continue the uptrend.

POI Identi?cation
In True SMC, a Point of Interest ?POI? is strictly defined as either a valid Order Flow ?OF? or a valid Order Block ?OB?.

Concepts such as Breaker Blocks ?BBs?, Mitigation Blocks ?MBs?, Fair Value Gaps ?FVGs?, Liquidity Voids, Vacuum Blocks, and similar formations are not considered POIs.

True SMC recognizes only two valid POI categories:

? Order Flows ?OF?

? Order Blocks ?OB?

A common mistake among traders is marking every visible Order Flow or Order Block as a potential entry zone. In reality, not all OFs and OBs carry the same significance.

Between a swing high and a swing low, or within a complete dealing range, there can only be:

? A minimum of one valid POI

? A maximum of two valid POIs These are classified as:
? Decisional POI ?First Choice)

? Extreme POI ?Last Line of Defense)

Any other OFs or OBs located within the same dealing range are considered weak zones and primarily serve as Smart Money Traps ?SMTs? designed to attract premature entries.
Decisional POI
The Decisional POI is the first zone expected to produce the ultimate reversal.

It represents the area directly responsible for the displacement that created the valid Break of Structure ?BOS?.

A Decisional POI can be:

? A Decisional Order Flow: The last opposing move before the displacement that creates the Break of Structure.

? A Decisional Order Block: The Order Block directly responsible for causing the Break of Structure.

Extreme POI

For this reason, the Extreme POI is often viewed as the market's final line of defense. An Extreme POI can be:
? An Extreme Order Flow: The furthest unmitigated Order Flow located at the origin of the dealing range.

? An Extreme Order Block: The furthest unmitigated Order Block contained within the Extreme Orderflow.

The Origin Order Block is the furthest unmitigated Order Block located at the origin of the dealing range.

This Order Block represents the earliest institutional footprint responsible for initiating the displacement that ultimately formed the dealing range.

The Origin Order Block only becomes relevant in rare scenarios where the original Extreme Orderflow at the origin of the dealing range has already been mitigated.

The untouched Origin Order Block within the original Orderflow that has already been partially mitigated remains valid as long as it has not been mitigated.

In the few cases where it is present, it represents the final point of interest, only considered after the Extreme Order Block has failed.

CHAPTER 3: RISK MANAGEMENT
Risk management is the foundation of long-term survival in trading. It does not matter how strong your strategy is, how accurate your entries are, or how well you understand your trading strategy-without proper risk management, consistency is impossible.

Markets are inherently uncertain, and there is no guarantee that any trade will win. In fact, losses are a natural and inevitable part of trading. The difference between success and failure lies in how those losses are controlled and managed over time.

Many traders focus entirely on finding the perfect setup, yet ignore the one factor that determines whether they stay in the game long enough to benefit from it.

In reality, trading success is not defined by how much you make on your best trades, but by how well you protect your account during inevitable drawdowns.

This is why risk management is not an optional skill-it is the core framework that supports every decision you make in the market.

Risk Per Trade
Risk per trade refers to the fixed amount of capital you are willing to lose on a single trade if the market invalidates your idea.

Risk is not adjusted based on emotion, confidence, or how "good? a setup looks.

The ideal risk per trade is not random, and it is not based on how confident you feel about a setup. It is determined by two key factors: statistical consistency and psychological stability.

From a statistical perspective, the goal of risk management is to ensure that losses remain small enough for the trading edge to play out over a large sample of trades.

The objective is not to eliminate losses-losses are inevitable. The objective is to ensure that no realistic losing streak can remove you from the game before your edge has the opportunity to work.
In trading, an edge is a statistical advantage that makes one outcome more likely than another over a series of trades.


68

An edge does not mean every trade wins. Instead, it means that over time, the average profits generated by winning trades exceed the average losses generated by losing trades.

The role of risk management is to protect capital long enough for that edge to express itself through consistent execution and a sufficient number of trades.

Even profitable systems experience losing streaks. If risk is too high, a relatively small sequence of losses can significantly damage the account before the edge has an opportunity to express itself.

From a psychological perspective, risk must also be small enough to keep emotional pressure under control. The larger the amount at risk, the more emotionally attached a trader becomes to the outcome of each trade. This often leads to hesitation or fear that later breaks execution consistency and ultimately destroys the edge of the system.

For this reason, proper risk management is best achieved through a fixed-risk model rather than a dynamic one.

With a fixed-risk model, every trade carries the same percentage risk regardless of confidence, emotions, recent results, or perceived setup quality. This creates consistency in execution and allows performance to be evaluated objectively.

A dynamic-risk approach often introduces subjectivity, where traders increase risk after winning streaks or on setups they "feel" strongly about, exposing themselves to unnecessary volatility and emotional decision-making.
A fixed risk of approximately 0.5% per trade strikes a balance between capital preservation and account growth. At this level, losses remain manageable while allowing the trader to participate fully in the market over the long term.

To put this into perspective, a trader risking 0.5% per trade would need an extremely large number of consecutive losses to cause catastrophic damage to the account. After 10 consecutive losses, the account would be down roughly 5%. Even after 20 consecutive losses, a significant portion of the account would still remain intact.

But realistically, who is comfortable taking 20 consecutive losses? Very few traders would be.
And that is precisely the point.


69

Long before the account reaches a dangerous level, the trader would already have sufficient information to stop, review performance, identify mistakes, reassess market conditions, or determine whether the trading edge is still being executed correctly.

Risk management is therefore not designed to help you survive endless losses. It is designed to buy you time. Time to learn, time to adapt, time to correct mistakes, and time for your edge to play out over a meaningful sample of trades.

A trader who risks too much often runs out of capital before they have the opportunity to understand what went wrong. A trader who risks appropriately can withstand setbacks, learn from them, and continue operating.

This is one of the greatest advantages of small fixed risk: it keeps the trader in the game long enough for experience, discipline, and consistency to compound.


Position Sizing
Now, do you remember the foundation we built earlier, where we learned about pips and lot sizes?

Well, this is exactly where that knowledge becomes important.

Up to this point, we have discussed risk per trade and why it should remain fixed. However, knowing how much you are willing to risk is only half of the equation. The next step is determining the correct position size that aligns with that risk.
To do that, we must combine three key elements: account size, risk per trade, and
stop-loss distance measured in pips.

Position sizing is the process of calculating the correct trade volume based on your account size, predefined risk per trade, and stop-loss distance (distance in pips between the entry price and the stop loss price).

Many traders make the mistake of choosing position size first and then adjusting the stop loss to fit that size.

In reality, the process should be the opposite. The stop loss should be placed where the trade idea becomes invalid, and the position size should then be adjusted to ensure the predefined risk is respected.

70

For example, a trader with a $10,000 account risking 0.5% per trade is willing to lose a maximum of $50 if the trade is invalidated. Whether the stop loss is 10 pips, 20 pips, or 50 pips away, the position size must be adjusted so that the maximum loss remains $50.

This is why position sizing is one of the most important skills in trading. It ensures consistency in risk exposure and prevents traders from unknowingly risking too much on one trade and too little on another.

The process always follows the same sequence:

1. Determine account size.
2. Define risk per trade.
3. Identify the stop-loss distance in pips.
4. Calculate the appropriate position size.

Only after these steps are completed should a trade be executed.

Position Size Calculation

Now that we understand what position sizing is and why it matters, the next step is learning how to calculate it.

Position size is determined using three pieces of information:

Risk-to-Reward Ratio & Win Rate
Now that we understand how much to risk and how to calculate the correct position size, the next step is understanding the relationship between Risk-to-Reward Ratio and Win Rate.

Risk-to-Reward Ratio ?R?R? measures how much a trader is willing to risk in order to potentially earn a certain return. It compares the potential loss on a trade to the potential reward.

For example:

? 1?1 R?R ? Risking 1 unit to potentially make 1 unit
? 1?2 R?R ? Risking 1 unit to potentially make 2 units
? 1?3 R?R ? Risking 1 unit to potentially make 3 units

Win Rate, on the other hand, refers to the percentage of trades that close in profit. For example:
? 50% Win Rate ? 5 wins out of every 10 trades
? 60% Win Rate ? 6 wins out of every 10 trades
? 40% Win Rate ? 4 wins out of every 10 trades

Many traders mistakenly believe that a high win rate automatically means profitability. This is not true.

Likewise, a trader winning only 40% of trades can be highly profitable if the average win is sufficiently larger than the average loss. This is because profitability is determined by the relationship between Risk-to-Reward Ratio and Win Rate, not by either factor in isolation.

In trading, expectancy is the mathematical expectation of a trading system over time. It represents the average amount a trader can expect to gain or lose per trade based on the combination of win rate and Risk-to-Reward Ratio.

Stop Loss Placement
No matter how good a setup looks, how confident you are, or how many times a similar setup has worked in the past, every trade must have a stop loss.
Why?

Because markets are inherently uncertain.

No trader, strategy, institution, or algorithm can predict the future with 100% accuracy. Every trade carries risk, and every trading idea has the possibility of being wrong. The stop loss is what defines that risk before the trade is taken.

Without a stop loss, a trader is no longer operating within a controlled risk environment. The potential loss becomes undefined, making proper position sizing and risk management impossible.

76

More importantly, the stop loss acts as a protective barrier between a normal losing trade and a catastrophic loss. A controlled loss is simply a business expense. An uncontrolled loss has the potential to erase weeks, months, or even years of progress.

Many traders accept a stop loss when entering a trade, only to move it further away when price approaches it. In reality, this is not avoiding a loss-it is increasing risk after the trade has already been placed. The market does not know or care where your stop loss is. If the trade idea has been invalidated, accepting the predefined loss is often the most professional decision a trader can make.

A trader's success is not measured by how often they avoid stop losses. It is measured by how consistently they respect them.

The purpose of a stop loss is not to prevent losing trades.

The purpose of a stop loss is to ensure that when losses occur, they remain small, controlled, and incapable of threatening the trader's long-term survival.


Common Risk Management Mistakes
Most traders do not fail because they lack a strategy. They fail because they fail to manage risk properly.
A profitable strategy combined with poor risk management will eventually lead to inconsistency, unnecessary drawdowns, and in many cases, account failure. For this reason, understanding the most common risk management mistakes is just as important as understanding the rules themselves.

Risking Too Much Per Trade

One of the most common mistakes traders make is risking an excessive percentage of their account on a single trade.

While larger risk may seem attractive because of the potential for faster growth, it also magnifies losses, emotional pressure, and account volatility. The objective is not to grow the account as quickly as possible, but to grow it sustainably.



77

Inconsistent Risk

Some traders risk 0.5% on one trade, 1% on another, and 3% on a setup they feel particularly confident about.

This creates inconsistency in performance and makes it difficult to accurately evaluate the effectiveness of a trading system. Risk should remain fixed and predefined regardless of emotions, confidence, or recent results.

Moving the Stop Loss

A stop loss should represent the point at which the trade idea becomes invalid.

Moving the stop loss further away simply because price is approaching it increases risk after the trade has already been placed. This behavior is often driven by hope rather than logic and can quickly lead to losses far greater than originally intended.

Trading Without a Stop Loss

Trading without a stop loss means trading with undefined risk.

Without a predefined exit point, a single trade has the potential to cause significant damage to the account. No matter how strong a setup appears, every trade must have a level where the idea is considered invalid.

Focusing on Profit Instead of Risk

Many traders become obsessed with how much they can make on a trade while paying little attention to how much they can lose.

Professional traders think differently. Before considering the reward, they first define the risk. Capital preservation always comes before profit generation.

Overtrading

Overtrading occurs when a trader takes too many trades outside of their structured trading plan.

This is often driven by impatience, boredom, or the desire to recover losses quickly. In reality, more trades do not mean more profitability. Instead, overtrading usually leads to lower-quality setups, reduced discipline, and unnecessary losses.


78

Revenge Trading

After experiencing a loss, some traders immediately attempt to recover it by taking impulsive trades or increasing risk.

This behavior often results in even larger losses and causes traders to deviate from their trading plan. Losses are a normal part of trading and should never be treated as something that must be recovered immediately.

Ignoring Position Sizing

Knowing the correct risk percentage is meaningless if position size is calculated incorrectly.

Many traders place valid stop losses but execute inappropriate position sizes, unintentionally risking more than planned. Proper position sizing is what converts risk management from theory into practice.



Risk management is not the most exciting part of trading, but it is one of the most important.

A trader can survive with an average strategy and exceptional risk management far longer than a trader with an exceptional strategy and poor risk management.

At the end of the day, opportunities in the market are endless. Capital is not.

Protecting capital is therefore not optional-it is the responsibility of every serious trader.
